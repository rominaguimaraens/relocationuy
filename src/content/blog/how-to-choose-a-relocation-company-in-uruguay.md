# How to Choose a Relocation Company in Uruguay

If you search for help moving to Uruguay, you will find several companies that look alike from the outside. They all promise to take care of your residency, they all have a friendly website, and most of them have a few lines about how much their clients love them. From a distance you can't tell how they actually work, and the difference shows up months later, in an appointment room, when something has to be fixed.

This guide is for anyone comparing companies, including us. The questions below work on every one of them. We have also listed what a good answer sounds like, so you can tell a real answer from a vague one.

---

## First, Do You Need Help at All?

You can apply for residency in Uruguay without an immigration lawyer. The standard process does not require one, and a tourist visa is enough to begin. If you speak Spanish and enjoy paperwork, you can do all of it yourself.

Most people don't want to, and one reason is practical. The DNM (the national immigration office) will not go ahead with an appointment if you can't communicate in Spanish, so unless you are fluent, you need someone in the room who is. The other reason is the sequence. Documents have to be gathered, translated and notarized in a certain order, and a document done at the wrong time or in the wrong place has to be done again. Help is worth paying for when it keeps you out of those loops.

So the question to ask a company is what, specifically, it does for you that you can't easily do alone. If the answer is "everything," keep asking until you get details.

## Eight Questions to Ask Before You Pay

### 1. Who will actually be at my appointment?

Ask for a name. You want a person who will physically sit next to you at the DNM and speak for you in Spanish, not a remote assistant or someone who "coordinates." Ask whether the person who answers your emails is the same person who goes to your appointments. In some companies the sales team and the field team never meet each other, and you find out which one you hired on the day.

A good answer: a first name, a short description of what that person does at the appointment, and a clear yes or no on whether it is the same person you have been talking to.

### 2. What exactly is included, and what is not?

Ask for the scope in writing, as a list. Then ask about the items people usually assume are covered: the health card and the medical exam, the notary who certifies your proof of income, the sworn translator, government fees, and the apostilles you need from your home country.

Some of these are paid to someone other than the company, and that is normal. What matters is that you know before you sign, so that your first month isn't a series of small bills you did not expect. Ask for a total, including every third-party cost you will pay yourself.

A good answer: a written list with an "included" side and a "not included" side, and a company that tells you readily what it does not do.

### 3. Who does the translations, and where?

The DNM only accepts translations done inside Uruguay by a Uruguayan certified sworn translator. A translation done in your home country, however good, will not be accepted. If a company tells you to get your documents translated before you arrive, ask them why.

Ask who the translator is, whether you deal with them directly, and who pays them. Both arrangements are common, but you should know which one you are in.

### 4. What happens if something is filed incorrectly?

An application is a stack of names, dates and spellings. If one of them is wrong, it can follow you onto your cédula, and correcting it afterwards takes time and more appointments. It matters especially if your name has changed. Trans clients whose legal name was updated before the move need that update to actually be submitted. If it isn't, the cédula can come out with the old name on it.

Ask who checks the application before it goes in, and who fixes a mistake and at whose expense.

A good answer: a clear account of who checks the application before it goes in, and a straight statement that the company corrects its own errors at its own cost.

### 5. How will I know where my process stands?

The residency process has long quiet stretches. The gap between being "in process" and official approval is the least predictable part of the whole thing, and it is where most people start to worry. A company should tell you what happens during those weeks and how you will hear about changes.

Ask whether there is a place you can check your status, how often they update you, and who to message when you are anxious at 11 pm. Daily messages aren't necessary. What helps is knowing that someone is watching your file.

### 6. How do you get paid, and what happens to my money?

Ask how the payment is split, what each payment is for, and what is refundable if you change your mind or the company can't deliver. Splitting the payment is common and reasonable, for example half when you book and half at the first appointment. Paying everything months in advance for a service that hasn't started deserves a second look.

### 7. Have you worked with people in my situation?

The residency process is the same for everyone, but some situations call for more care from the person helping you. That might be a family traveling with children, an older client who isn't comfortable with technology, or an LGBTQ+ person whose documents need extra attention.

Ask whether the company has worked with people in your situation and what that looked like. A specific answer is better than "yes, of course."

### 8. Does the person recommending you get paid for it?

Many of the reviews, forum replies and social posts that recommend a relocation company come from people who earn a commission when you sign. This is legal and common, and some of those recommendations are also honest. You should still know about it. If you are choosing based on someone's glowing post, ask them directly whether they are paid for referrals.

## Warning Signs

A few things come up often enough to be worth listing.

- **A guaranteed date.** Nobody controls the DNM. A company can tell you how long things usually take, and they can tell you which parts are predictable. A promise of an exact approval date is a sales line.
- **"You need a lawyer."** For a standard residency, you don't. If a company says you do, ask what the lawyer will do that the company can't.
- **Pressure to pay everything now.** A good company is happy to give you time to think, and the real work starts after you book.
- **Vagueness about who does the work.** If you can't get a name, a role and a description of what happens at the appointment, treat that as the answer.
- **No written scope.** If the services are not on paper, you will find out what was included only when something is missing.
- **Defensiveness when you ask about mistakes.** Every company makes some. What counts is how they handle them.
